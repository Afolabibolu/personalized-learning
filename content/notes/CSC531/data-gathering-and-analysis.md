# Data Gathering and Data Analysis

## What the topic is

Establishing requirements is not writing a wish list of features. Data gathering collects enough information for stable requirements and, later, captures users' reactions and performance during evaluation. Data analysis then turns what was gathered into findings.

## Subtopics

**Key issues.** Set goals first, because they decide the technique. Identify participants (sampling). Use a consent form: informed consent is required. Use **triangulation**: methodological (combining techniques), data (different sources, times and places), investigator (different researchers) and theories (different frameworks). Run a pilot study.

**Sampling.** Probability sampling (random generator, every nth person, stratified) lets you apply statistical tests and generalise to the whole population. Non-probability sampling (convenience sampling, volunteer panels) does not give such robust generalisation.

**Pilot studies.** A small trial of the main study, to check the idea is viable and to clear up ambiguity. People in the pilot are typically not in the main study. In one example, 17 reviewers tried a survey in Pidgin English; it was revised into English with GIFs plus a Pidgin version, and 1,267 of 1,533 responses came through the English version.

**Techniques.** Interviews are usually synchronous: structured (when goals are clear), semi-structured or unstructured (exploratory, rich but slow to analyse). Focus groups have about 3 to 10 people and a facilitator who keeps the discussion balanced. Questionnaires are usually asynchronous and can reach a wider group. They cover demographics and background, use non-overlapping ranges (15–20, 21–25) and rating scales (Likert and semantic differential). Observation is direct (in the field or a usability lab, often with think-aloud) or indirect (diaries, interaction logs, web analytics). Data can be recorded as notes and photos (least technical), audio (lets the interviewer focus) or video (more intrusive, needs planning).

**Analysis.** Open-ended questions give qualitative data; fixed options give quantitative data. Simple statistics include percentages (use them with at least 10 users). Charts include bar charts, histograms, pie charts and scatter diagrams; scatter diagrams show outliers easily. Qualitative analysis looks for recurring themes, through thematic analysis or affinity diagrams. Inferential analysis tests whether an independent variable (for example mouse or trackpad) affects a dependent variable (time, errors). Cronbach's alpha (0 to 1) measures the internal consistency, or reliability, of survey items.

## Key terms

- **Dependent variable:** what you measure. **Independent variable:** what you change.
- **Triangulation:** using more than one perspective to check findings.

## Exam-style point

"Which interface (command line, desktop metaphor, virtual environment) is quickest?" The independent variable is interface type, with three levels. The dependent variable is task completion time.
