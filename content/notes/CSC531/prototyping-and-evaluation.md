# Prototyping, Design Principles and Evaluation

## What the topic is

A prototype makes a design concrete enough to test. Evaluation collects and analyses data about users' experiences with a design artefact, with the central goal of improving it. Designers who assume that "if we can use it, others can too" are often wrong, which is why evaluation is integral to the design process.

## Subtopics

**Prototypes.** Low-fidelity prototypes are cheap: paper is the cheapest, with computer-generated sketches in tools like GIMP or Draw.io. A high-fidelity prototype is a computer-based interactive system close to the final product. It is built with tools such as Figma and Adobe XD, or HTML, CSS, JavaScript and Android Studio. It tests UI details, animations and navigation, shows the user experience, gathers feedback before launch and helps communicate with clients and team members.

**Why, what, when and where to evaluate.** Products with good experiences sell, and problems found before release are cheaper to fix. You can evaluate a low-tech prototype, a complete system, one screen, a workflow, the aesthetics or safety features. Early evaluation after sketches and storyboards is **formative**; later evaluation of a high-fidelity or finished product is **summative**. Settings are controlled (a lab), natural (real life) or remote.

**Three categories of evaluation.**
- **Controlled settings directly involving users:** usability labs, with users' activities controlled to test hypotheses. The main methods are usability testing and experiments.
- **Natural settings involving users:** field studies, with little or no control. They are realistic but time-consuming.
- **Settings not directly involving users:** experts critique and model the interface. Methods are heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthroughs (often using hierarchical task analysis) and analytics.

**Nielsen's heuristics.** Visibility of what is going on, minimise memory load, be consistent, provide feedback, clearly marked exits, deal with errors helpfully, flexibility and efficiency, aesthetic and minimalist design, help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors, and help and documentation. Heuristic evaluation is done by experienced experts; the number of evaluators matters, and bias or inexperience can mislead.

**Shneiderman's eight golden rules.** Strive for consistency, seek universal usability, offer informative feedback, design dialogues to yield closure, prevent errors, permit easy reversal of actions, keep users in control and reduce short-term memory load.

**Usability testing.** It targets end users and combines observation, interviews and questionnaires: errors, time on task, satisfaction. In think aloud, recruit representative users, give them representative tasks, then "shut up and let the users do the talking". Diaries and interaction logs give indirect, longitudinal data. Experiments test a null hypothesis against an alternative, often with a t-test. Brooke's System Usability Scale gives a quantitative score.

## Key terms

- **Formative vs summative:** evaluation during design vs of the finished product.
- **Heuristic evaluation:** experts checking a design against rules of thumb.

## Exam-style point

"Why are both formative and summative evaluations needed?" Formative evaluation finds problems early, while changes are cheap. Summative evaluation confirms that the finished product meets its goals with real users.
