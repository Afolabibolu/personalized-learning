# Understanding the Problem Space and User-Centred Design

## What the topic is

It is tempting to start designing an interactive product from its interface, but doing so often neglects usability issues and the user experience. This topic is about understanding the problem space first, then designing with users at the centre.

## Subtopics

**Understanding the problem space.** In 2018 a team set out to solve crime in the Havanna community in Namibia. Being "techy people", they first thought of a crime-reporting app. After visiting the community, analysing responses and ranking the severity of crime-related issues with residents, the problem was refined to questions like how to get school dropouts back to school, reduce alcohol abuse and keep unemployed offenders busy. Useful probes: are there problems with an existing product, why, how would your design overcome them, and how would it support, change or extend current ways of doing things?

**Why it matters.** Understanding the problem gives a clear reason for the design, saves time, effort and money later, and lets ill-thought-out or unusable ideas be refined while it is still easy. In a nutshell, understanding the problem means understanding users.

**Dot voting.** Participants place dots on the items they feel strongly about. They may have several votes (3 to 5) and can put them all on one item. In Havanna it narrowed the ideas to three: drones and cameras, mentorship, and business competitions.

**Levels of engagement.** The team met the community leader (unguided conversation, 1–2 hours), then 10 community members (structured interview guide, 3–4 hours), then 3–4 selected members (focus group discussion, a full day). Each level gave deeper understanding.

**Approaches to interaction design.** User-centred design: the user knows best and guides the designer. Activity-centred design: users' behaviour around tasks matters more than their goals. System design: the system is the centre of attention. Genius design: relies on the designer's experience and creative flair.

**User-centred design.** Its basis is early focus on users and tasks, empirical measurement and iterative design. Its steps: identify users' needs and context, specify business and user requirements, design solutions, evaluate. Ask "What technologies are available to support users' goals?", not "Where can we deploy this new technology?"

**User profiles, personas and scenarios.** Primary users use the system directly; secondary users use it infrequently or through an intermediary; tertiary users are affected by it. A persona is a fictitious yet realistic user created from the profile, early in design. A scenario is a short story containing the task the user wants to perform.

## Key terms

- **Persona:** a fictional but realistic model of a typical user.
- **Card sorting:** users organise topics into categories (design phase).

## Exam-style point

"Who are the stakeholders for a home electricity smart meter?" Answer with the three user types: household members (primary), people who use it rarely or through someone else (secondary), and those affected by it, such as the electricity company (tertiary).
