# Thermal Expansion

## Linear expansion

A rod of length L₀ heated through ΔT grows by **ΔL = αL₀ΔT**, where α is the **linear expansivity** (K⁻¹ or °C⁻¹). For example, a steel rod (α = 1 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹) that grows by 5 mm over 10 °C was 5 × 10⁻³/(10⁻⁵ × 10) = **50 m** long.

For copper wire 35 m long (α = 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹ is the usual value), a 55 °C rise adds about 3.3 cm.

## Area and volume

- Area: **ΔA = βA₀ΔT with β = 2α**. A 4.5 cm square hole in aluminium (α = 2.3 × 10⁻⁵) heated by 120 °C grows by 2 × 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ × 120 = 0.0055 = **0.55%**. Holes expand **as if they were filled with the material**.
- Volume: **ΔV = γV₀ΔT with γ = 3α**. A pot of 100 cm³ growing by 0.6 cm³ over 80 °C has γ = 7.5 × 10⁻⁵, so **α = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ /°C**.

## Density

Mass is unchanged while volume grows, so **density falls** on heating: ρ = ρ₀/(1 + γΔT). An iron bar heated from 283 K to 383 K expands and becomes less dense.

## Thermal stress

If a rod is **prevented** from expanding, the strain it would have had is ΔL/L₀ = αΔT. The stress is then **Yα ΔT**, and the force is **F = YAαΔT**. For a steel beam (Y = 2 × 10¹¹ Pa, α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵, A = 200 cm²) heated 25 °C, F = 2 × 10¹¹ × 0.02 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 25 = **1.2 × 10⁶ N**. Gaps are left in railways and bridges for this reason.

## Liquids

Liquids have only volume expansivity. The **apparent** expansion seen in a container equals the real expansion minus the container's expansion. Water is unusual: it **contracts** from 0 °C to 4 °C, so its density is greatest at **4 °C**, which is why ponds freeze from the top.
