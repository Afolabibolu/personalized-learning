# Heat Capacity, Latent Heat and Calorimetry

## Heat capacity

The heat needed to raise a mass m by ΔT is **Q = mcΔT**, where c is the **specific heat capacity** (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). For water c = 4185 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, which is high. The **heat capacity** of a whole body is C = mc.

## Latent heat

At a change of state the temperature stays **constant** while heat goes into breaking bonds: **Q = mL**.

- Latent heat of **fusion** of ice: **L_f = 335 kJ/kg** (3.35 × 10⁵ J/kg).
- Latent heat of **vaporisation** of water: **L_v = 2260 kJ/kg**.

**Electrical heating**: a heater of power P supplies **Q = Pt**. Melting 10 g of ice needs 0.01 × 3.35 × 10⁵ = 3350 J, so a 2 W heater takes 1675 s. (The typed paper's L of 3350 J/g is ten times the true value.)

## Calorimetry

In an insulated mixture, **heat lost by the hot parts = heat gained by the cold parts**.

**Example**: a hot liquid at 80 °C is added to 600 g of the same liquid at 10 °C, and the mixture settles at 30 °C. Then m × 50 = 600 × 20, so m = 240 g and the total is **840 g**. The specific heat capacity cancels.

**Example with ice**: to cool 500 g of water from 20 °C to 0 °C needs 0.5 × 4185 × 20 = 41.9 kJ removed. Ice at −10 °C (c_ice ≈ 2100 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) absorbs 21 kJ/kg to warm to 0 °C and 335 kJ/kg to melt, 356 kJ/kg in all. So about **0.12 kg** of ice is needed.

## Method of mixtures

The **calorimeter** itself also takes or gives heat: include its term m_cal c_cal ΔT. Stirring, lagging and quick work reduce losses. Cooling corrections allow for heat lost to the surroundings.

Specific heat capacities of gases are discussed with the first law: C_p − C_v = R per mole for an ideal gas.
