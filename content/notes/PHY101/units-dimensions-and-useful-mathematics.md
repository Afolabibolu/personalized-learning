# Units, Dimensions and Useful Mathematics

## Quantities and units

There are **two groups** of measurable quantities. **Fundamental** quantities depend on no other quantity; **derived** quantities are built from them. The **seven fundamental quantities** and their SI units are:

- mass: **kilogram**;
- length: **metre**;
- time: **second**;
- temperature: **kelvin**;
- electric current: **ampere**;
- amount of substance: **mole**;
- luminous intensity: **candela**.

Derived units include the **newton** (kg m s⁻²), the **joule** (kg m² s⁻²) and the **watt** (J/s).

Prefixes run from femto (10⁻¹⁵), pico, nano, micro and milli up to kilo, mega, giga, tera and peta (10¹⁵). A nucleus is about 1 fm across; a human hair is about 100 μm thick.

## Dimensions

The **dimension** of a quantity shows how it is built from the fundamental quantities. In mechanics everything comes from **M, L and T**. Common dimensions:

- velocity **LT⁻¹**; acceleration **LT⁻²**;
- force **MLT⁻²**; energy **ML²T⁻²**;
- pressure ML⁻¹T⁻²; density ML⁻³.

From E = hc/λ, Planck's constant has dimension **ML²T⁻¹**. The dimension of a quantity does not change whatever unit is used. **Dimensionless** quantities (pure numbers) can still have units: angles in radians, for example.

## Using dimensions

1. **Checking equations**: every term of a valid equation has the same dimension. In v² = u² + 2as, each term is L²T⁻².
2. **Deriving relations**: suppose a pendulum's period is T = k lˣ mʸ gᶻ. Matching powers gives x = ½, y = 0 and z = −½, so **T = k√(l/g)**, where the constant turns out to be 2π.

For x = At² + B, A is LT⁻² and B is L. In vⁿ = 2ax the dimensions match only if **n = 2**.

## Useful mathematics

- **Logarithms** turn power laws into straight lines. PV^γ = C gives log P = −γ log V + log C, so a graph of **log P against log V** has slope −γ and intercept log C.
- **Derivatives** give rates: if x = ut + ½at², then dx/dt = u + at and **d²x/dt² = a**.
- **Integration** is continuous summation: adding rings 2πx dx from 0 to r gives the circle's area πr².
- **Sine and cosine** are sinusoidal, periodic functions; cosine leads sine by π/2.
