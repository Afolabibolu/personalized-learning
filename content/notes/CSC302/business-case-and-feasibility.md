# The Business Case and Feasibility

## The business case and strategic planning

The **business case** is the reasons, or justification, for a proposal. A strong business case shows that the company should pursue the alternative because it is in the firm's best interest. Systems development typically starts with a **systems request**, followed by a **preliminary investigation** that includes a **feasibility study**.

**Strategic planning** lets a company examine its purpose, vision and values and develop a **mission statement**. That leads to goals, objectives, day-to-day operations and business results. A **SWOT analysis** examines a company's **Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats**. The SDLC is the framework that turns strategic plans into systems.

## Systems requests

Systems projects are started to:

- improve performance;
- provide more information;
- reduce costs;
- strengthen controls;
- provide better service.

They are affected by **internal factors** (the strategic plan, top managers, user requests, the IT department, existing systems and data) and **external factors** (technology, suppliers, customers, competitors, the economy and government).

Large companies use a **systems review committee** to evaluate requests and set priorities; smaller ones often rely on one person.

## Feasibility

A systems request must pass several tests, together called a **feasibility study**:

- **Operational feasibility:** will the system be used effectively once it is developed?
- **Technical feasibility:** does the company have, or can it get, the resources to develop and run it?
- **Economic feasibility:** do the projected benefits outweigh the costs? This includes the **total cost of ownership (TCO)** and both **tangible benefits** (measurable in money) and **intangible benefits** (such as better morale or customer satisfaction).
- **Schedule feasibility:** can it be done in an acceptable time?

The first step is to weed out requests that are not feasible. Even a feasible request may not be necessary, and feasibility analysis continues throughout development.

**Setting priorities:** Will the proposed system reduce costs? Will it increase revenue? Where, when, how and how much?

## The preliminary investigation

1. Understand the problem or opportunity. A **fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram** helps here by tracing causes and effects.
2. Define the project scope and constraints.
3. Perform fact-finding: analyse organisation charts, conduct interviews, review documentation, observe operations and conduct a user survey.
4. Analyse project usability, cost, benefit and schedule data.
5. Evaluate feasibility: operational, technical, economic and schedule.
6. Present results and recommendations to management.

The end product is a report with these sections:

- introduction;
- systems request summary;
- findings;
- case for action;
- project roles;
- time and cost estimates;
- expected benefits;
- appendix.
