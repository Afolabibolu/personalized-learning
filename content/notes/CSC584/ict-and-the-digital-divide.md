# ICT and the Digital Divide

## What ICT is

**Information and communication technologies (ICT)** are a diverse set of technological tools and resources used to transmit, store, create, share or exchange information (UIS, 2009). They include:

- computers and the Internet (websites, blogs, email);
- live broadcasting (radio, television, webcasting);
- recorded broadcasting (podcasts, audio and video players, storage devices);
- telephony (fixed, mobile, satellite, video-conferencing).

There is no single universal definition, because ICT keeps evolving. It covers old technologies such as landlines, radio and TV alongside the Internet, IoT, the metaverse, virtual reality, social media, cloud computing, AI, robotics, 5G/6G, Web3 and quantum computing.

**ICT versus IT.** ICT is the more comprehensive term. IT is about managing information technologies (software, hardware and networking) and does **not** include telecommunications, which ICT does. IT is a subset of ICT.

**Components of ICT:** devices (hardware), software, middleware, data, wired and wireless networks, communication technologies, the cloud, protocols and interfaces, and information security and governance policies. ICT also means the *application* of these components, which is where its real power and danger lie.

## Benefits and challenges

For business, ICT brings automated processes that cut costs, the big data revolution, and ICT-enabled transactions such as online shopping, telemedicine and social media. Its challenges include cybercrime against enterprises, governments and critical infrastructure; automation and robots displacing workers in repetitive jobs; and social problems such as trolling, cyberbullying, isolation, loneliness and depression.

## The digital age and the digital divide

ICT is credited with the **Fourth Industrial Revolution** and the move from face-to-face to digital interaction, known as the **digital age**. But access is uneven: richer countries and individuals enjoy more of it. That gap is the **digital divide**. Governments and NGOs try to bridge it by widening affordable access.

## Developmental characteristics of ICTs

- ICTs are **tools**: their value depends on fit, incentives and skills.
- They apply across almost every sector.
- They are multifunctional and interoperable.
- Innovation makes them cheaper and easier to use.
- **Knowledge management:** they help collect, store, retrieve and share knowledge.
- **Efficiency:** they help public and private bodies, including SMEs, work at lower cost.
- **Networks:** they give equal access to information and markets.
- **Multipurpose:** they are easy to adapt.
- They are intertwined with **globalisation**.

## ICTs as enablers

ICTs drive **economic and productivity growth**, especially with organisational change; rural trading networks in Africa and India are an example. They enable **social development** in healthcare (remote consultation, epidemics), education (distance learning) and the environment (monitoring). They support **good governance** through e-government, transparency and oversight. They also form an **industrial sector** of hardware, software, services and software-plus-services.

**Factors affecting ICT-driven development:**
- policy: intellectual property, property rights, trade, competition, research, security and privacy, standards, e-government;
- **human capacity**: education, digital literacy and skills;
- **enterprise**: access to capital and investment incentives;
- **infrastructure and access**.
