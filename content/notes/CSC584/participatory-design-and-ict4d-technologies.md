# Participatory Design and ICT4D Technologies

## Participatory design

**Participatory design** is a collaborative approach in which real users and stakeholders take part in the design process with professional designers. It is used in software development, architecture, urban planning and healthcare. It began in Germany and became popular in Scandinavia in the 1980s before spreading to North America.

**Key principles:**
- focus on the user;
- **mutual learning** between designers and stakeholders, where everyone's opinion carries weight;
- empowerment;
- inclusiveness;
- stages, small agile-like iterations toward bigger goals;
- respect.

**Pros:** it lowers costs and raises the chance of immediate user satisfaction, avoiding expensive rework. **Downsides:** time, sample size, structure and quality. It can be expensive and slow.

**To implement it:** recruit participants, define goals, set up a structure, leverage divergence and convergence, and follow up with participants. It continues throughout the project.

## e-Health

The WHO defines **e-health** as the use of ICT in support of health and health-related fields. **Digital health** is the wider umbrella, covering e-health (including m-health), big data, genomics and AI. Its building blocks include:
- **electronic health records (EHRs)**;
- **m-health**, using mobile devices;
- **sensors**: non-invasive, wearable and invasive;
- **telehealth**, self-care and personalised care.

The EU e-Health action plan (2012–2020) listed these barriers:
- low awareness of and confidence in e-health among patients and professionals;
- lack of interoperability;
- limited large-scale evidence of cost-effectiveness;
- inadequate legal frameworks, including no reimbursement schemes;
- high start-up costs.

## e-Government

Governments use ICT to work more efficiently and transparently, to let citizens reach records and services online, and to support democratic participation. **Factors affecting e-government:**
1. **National context:** the political system, level of economic development, culture and history.
2. **Political and institutional factors:** governance structures.
3. **Socio-economic and cultural factors:** values and beliefs that shape adoption.
4. **Technical infrastructure:** hardware, software, telecoms and human resources.

## Information infrastructures

From a technical view, an **information infrastructure** consists of standards, wires and settings that link elements and codify their interactions. Infrastructures grow by **scaling**. **Standards** are nested inside one another, unevenly distributed, and relative to communities of practice: one person's good standard may be another's nightmare. They also embody ethics and values.

## Geographic information systems (GIS)

Putting information into a GIS is **data capture**. Digital tables and satellite images are uploaded directly; maps are scanned first. There are two main formats:
- **raster:** grids of cells or pixels, for data that varies, such as elevation or satellite imagery;
- **vector:** points (nodes), lines and polygons, for data with firm borders, such as streets or school districts.

All sources must be aligned to the same **scale**. GIS is used to choose shop locations, compare population with resources such as drinking water, track animal migration and plan disaster response, showing which neighbourhoods are in danger, where to put shelters and which routes are safe.
