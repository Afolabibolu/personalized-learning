# Developmental Informatics and Big Data

## Development informatics

Informatics studies how information is transformed, processed and communicated. **Development informatics** focuses on the role of ICTs in **sustainable socio-economic development** and on their contribution to public services. It is becoming more strategic under the UN's 17 **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**, which replaced the Millennium Development Goals after 2015.

## The two-fold core of big data

1. **New sources of data.** The **digital footprint** of every digital communication and transaction can replace traditional sources, such as surveys, with cheap, real-time proxy indicators. Google used 50 million common search terms as a proxy for the spread of seasonal flu.
2. **Methods of analytics.** Big data puts analytic treatment of data at the front of intelligent decision-making, whatever the scale in giga-, tera- or petabytes. This is the step from information societies to **knowledge societies**. Google tested 450 million models and found 45 search terms that predicted flu outbreaks in real time.

Because reality changes, the best big data can do is update estimates in real time, called **nowcasting**. Data always comes from the past, so it predicts well only when the future follows the same logic. This is the **Lucas critique** in economics, Goodhart's law in finance and Campbell's law in education.

## Goals of ICT4D

Development work explicitly aims to create a future **different from the past** and to change how the system operates. Outcomes are context-dependent and unique; a developing Africa is not an extrapolation of Europe's past. That limits the value of past data.

## Big data for development

Big data has been called "the new oil". The OECD calls it a core economic asset. The UN says new technologies create unprecedented possibilities. The UN Economic and Social Council reported that big data can produce more relevant and timely statistics than surveys.

**Five characteristics of big data:**
1. **Universal sampling:** it tries to capture everything (n = N). Mobile phones, with over 95% global penetration, are a key source.
2. **Real-time** access.
3. A **low-cost digital footprint** produced anyway.
4. **Messy and incomplete**, compensated for by **data fusion** across redundant sources. The Thomson Reuters MarketPsych Indices combine social media and news into over 18,000 fine-grained indices.
5. **The end of theory:** pattern detection by data mining and machine learning. Google Translate was built without linguists.

## Opportunities and challenges

**Opportunity:** combining big data with **agent-based modelling** (e-science) to simulate interventions before they happen. The Portland simulation modelled 1.6 million residents to test light-rail routes.

**Challenges:** new technology is never automatically good. Digitising 20 million land records in Bangalore mostly helped large players, deepening inequality.
- **Access:** footprints miss the unconnected. In Rwanda (2005–2009, 2–20% penetration), phones were mostly owned by the privileged. The bandwidth divide keeps evolving.
- **Human resources and skills:** Google's Hal Varian called statistician "the sexy job" of the coming years.
