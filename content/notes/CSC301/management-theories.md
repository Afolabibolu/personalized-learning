# Management Theories

## Classical: scientific management (Frederick Taylor, 1856–1917)

Taylor applied scientific methods to work. He proposed:

- a clearly defined daily task for each worker;
- standard conditions (work and motion studies);
- high pay for completing tasks;
- scientific selection, education and development of workers;
- close cooperation between management and workers, with managers taking more supervisory responsibility.

Its assumptions were that scientific analysis improves results, that the focus is on the work and not the person, and that each worker is an **economic man** motivated by money.

- **Benefits:** tasks could be measured, working methods and physical conditions improved, productivity rose, pay by results became possible, and it founded modern work study.
- **Disadvantages:** workers had no discretion; jobs became fragmented and boring; a "carrot and stick" approach; planning and control lay only with management; and social needs were overlooked.

## Classical: administrative theory (Henri Fayol, 1841–1925)

Fayol, a French engineer, is the **father of classical organization theory**. He defined management as planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating and controlling, and listed **14 principles**:

1. division of work;
2. authority and responsibility;
3. discipline;
4. **unity of command** (one superior);
5. unity of direction (one head, one plan);
6. subordination of individual interest to the general interest;
7. remuneration;
8. centralization;
9. **scalar chain** (line of authority);
10. order;
11. equity;
12. stability of tenure of personnel;
13. initiative;
14. **esprit de corps** ("in union there is strength").

## Classical: bureaucracy (Max Weber, 1864–1920)

Weber, a German sociologist, described **bureaucracy** to reduce the abuse of power: an organizational form of rules and a hierarchy of authority. It is specialized, rational, professional, impersonal and stable, with positions defined in writing.

- **Advantages:** it suits stable, routine environments.
- **Weaknesses:** rules become obstacles ("red tape"), it discourages innovation, gives rigid service, is hard to change, and treats people as passive.

## Neo-classical: human relations (Elton Mayo, 1880–1949)

Mayo's **Hawthorne studies** at Western Electric found that:

- workers must be seen as members of groups;
- the need to belong and have status matters more than money or working conditions;
- **informal groups** strongly influence behaviour;
- supervisors must cater for social needs.

Its weaknesses are that it suggested one best way again, oversimplified human motives, and assumed that satisfied workers are always productive.

## Assumptions about people

**Edgar Schein** described four views of people:

1. **rational-economic** (motivated by self-interest, from Adam Smith);
2. **social** (from Mayo);
3. **self-actualizing** (from Maslow);
4. **complex** (motives vary, and the relationship is a psychological contract).

**Douglas McGregor** proposed two views:

- **Theory X:** people dislike work, must be coerced and controlled, avoid responsibility and want security.
- **Theory Y:** work is as natural as play; people exercise self-direction, seek responsibility and are creative.

Theory X matches Schein's rational-economic man and Theory Y the self-actualizing man; in practice a blend is found.
