# Syllables and Stress

## Suprasegmentals

The spoken form of a language can be studied from two angles: the **segmental** (vowels and consonants) and the **suprasegmental**, which deals with **syllable, stress, rhythm and intonation**, the features above the segment.

## The syllable

The **syllable** is the **smallest pronounceable unit** of a language and the domain of the other suprasegmentals. Do not confuse it with the **morpheme**, the **smallest meaningful unit**, although they sometimes coincide (boy, read). Words are **monosyllabic** (boys, house), **disyllabic** (ru-ler, doc-tor) or **trisyllabic** (me-lo-dy, a-ban-don), and so on.

A syllable has three parts: the **onset** (consonants before the vowel), the **nucleus or peak** (the vowel) and the **coda** (consonants after it). **Only the nucleus is obligatory.** In some unstressed final syllables the nucleus is a **syllabic consonant**, /m/, /n/ or /l/, as in **rhythm**, **Britain** and **bottle**. English allows at most **three consonants at the onset** and **four at the coda**.

## Stress

**Stress** is the **prominence** placed on a syllable relative to others in a word. **Content words** (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) of one syllable are stressed; **grammatical words** (prepositions, articles, conjunctions) usually are not. A stressed syllable is marked by **pitch, length, vowel quality and loudness**; **schwa /ə/ is never stressed**. English stress is **free**, not fixed as in French, so the pronunciation of each word should be learnt with its meaning. There are **primary, secondary and tertiary** degrees of stress, and primary stress is marked with a stroke before the syllable, as in a'ccept.

## Patterns

- **Two-syllable words**: verbs often stress the **second** syllable (a'ccept, a'pply), while many nouns and adjectives stress the **first** ('money, 'clever).
- **Three-syllable verbs**: the last syllable is stressed only if it has a **long vowel or diphthong** or ends in two consonants (enter'tain); otherwise not (en'courage, de'termine).

**Suffixes** affect stress in three ways:

- They **take the stress**: **-ee** (refu'gee), **-eer** (engi'neer), **-aire** (millio'naire), **-ese** (Japa'nese), **-ette** (ciga'rette).
- They **shift the stress**: **-ion** (edu'cation), **-ic** (cli'matic), **-ity** (responsi'bility), **-graphy** (pho'tography), **-ian** (co'median).
- They **leave it unchanged**: **-ing**, **-ish**, **-ful**, **-age**, **-ment** ('useful, 'writing).

**Word-class pairs** change part of speech by moving the stress: the noun **'record** and the verb **re'cord**, or the noun **'export** and the verb **ex'port**. Some pairs keep the same stress, such as 'comment.

**Compounds** of two nouns usually stress the **first** element ('classroom, 'sunrise). Adverbial compounds stress the second (down'stream), as do compounds beginning with a number (first-'class), and verbs whose first element is an adverb stress the final item (up'grade, over'look).
