# Intonation and Rhythm

## Intonation

When people speak, their voices rise and fall. The range of the voice is **pitch**, and when pitch varies we have **intonation**. Each person has their own pitch range. There are **four major forms of intonation**: **rise**, **fall**, **rise-fall** and **fall-rise**.

## The tone-unit

The **tone-unit** (tone-group or sense-unit) is the domain of intonation. Phonological units are hierarchical: **phoneme, syllable, foot, tone-unit and utterance**. A tone-unit has four parts:

- the **pre-head (PH)**: unstressed syllables before the first stressed syllable;
- the **head (H)**: from the first stressed syllable;
- the **tonic syllable (TS)**: the **only obligatory part**, which carries the tone and the **tonic or nuclear stress**, usually on the **last lexical word**; and
- the **tail (T)**: syllables after the tonic syllable.

## Functions of intonation

1. **Grammatical**: intonation marks **boundaries** between phrases, clauses and sentences; shows **sentence type**, since **yes/no questions** usually take a **rise** ("Are you ready?") and **wh-questions** a **fall** ("Where did you keep it?"); and **disambiguates**: "Those who passed | immediately gained admission" differs from "Those who passed immediately | gained admission".
2. **Attitudinal**: tunes show emotion. A **rise** is used for general questions, encouragement and **listing**; when listing, the last item has a fall. A **fall** shows **finality** ("That's my conclusion"). A **rise-fall** shows surprise or being impressed. A **fall-rise** shows **uncertainty** or a polite **request**.
3. **Accentual (contrastive)**: the tonic stress is placed on a syllable for contrast, as in "She bought a **red** car" (not a blue one).
4. **Discourse**: intonation shows **new** and **given** information; in "The **bird** stays in this nest", bird is the new information.

## Rhythm

**Rhythm** is the recurrence of events at roughly regular intervals in fluent speech. If **syllables** recur at regular intervals, the language is **syllable-timed**, like **Igbo, Yoruba and most Nigerian languages**. If **stresses** recur regularly, the language is **stress-timed**, like **English**.

In stress-timing, the unit of rhythm is the **foot**: a stressed syllable and all the unstressed syllables up to the next stress. Feet are roughly **isochronous** (equal in time), so unstressed syllables are compressed. "The 'man has 'gone" has **5 syllables but 2 feet**.

To keep this rhythm, grammatical words in unstressed positions take **weak forms**: **to** /tə/, **from** /frəm/, **can** /kən/, **some** /səm/, **and** /ən/. Their **strong forms**, such as /tuː/ and /kæn/, are used when they are stressed.

Because English is a second language for many Nigerians and Nigerian languages are syllable-timed and tonal, these features must be learnt deliberately; listening to colloquial English and using a good dictionary help.
