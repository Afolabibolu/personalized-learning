# The English Sound System

## Speaking and the articulators

**Speaking** is a **productive skill**: the ability to produce a continuous flow of meaningful sound. Of the four language skills, **listening and speaking are primary** and reading and writing secondary. Learning English sounds helps **ESL (English as a Second Language)** speakers pronounce words correctly and be internationally intelligible.

Speech sounds are made by the **human articulators** (organs of speech). The **lungs** provide air, by the **pulmonic egressive airstream**; the **trachea** carries it; the **vocal cords** in the larynx vibrate for **voiced** sounds and stay apart for **voiceless** ones; and the **glottis** is the space between them. Other articulators are the **pharynx**, **epiglottis**, **oral** and **nasal cavities**, **lips**, **teeth**, **alveolar ridge**, **hard palate**, **soft palate (velum)**, **uvula** and **tongue** (tip, blade, front and back).

## Vowels

In vowels there is **no obstruction**, or the least possible, to the flow of air. **English has 20 vowels**, described by the tongue's height (close to open) and position (front, central or back), the **shape of the lips** (spread, neutral or rounded), **length** (long or short) and the **nucleus**.

- **Pure vowels (monophthongs)** have a **single vowel nucleus**; there are **12**: /iː/ (bee), /ɪ/ (sit), /e/ (set), /æ/ (man), /ɑː/ (pass), /ɒ/ (doll), /ɔː/ (sport), /ʊ/ (full), /uː/ (moon), /ʌ/ (jump), /ɜː/ (bird) and /ə/ (**schwa**, as in alone).
- **Diphthongs** are made when the tongue **glides from one vowel position to another** (a double nucleus); there are **8**: the closing diphthongs /eɪ/ (take), /əʊ/ (goal), /aɪ/ (try), /aʊ/ (how) and /ɔɪ/ (boy), and the centring diphthongs /ɪə/ (here), /eə/ (care) and /ʊə/ (poor).
- **Triphthongs** combine a closing diphthong with /ə/, as in **payer**, **mower**, **fire**, **flour** and **royalty**; they are not numbered among the 20.

## Consonants

In consonants there is a **significant obstruction** to the airflow. **English has 24 consonants**, described by:

1. **State of the glottis**: **voiced** (the cords vibrate, as in /b/, /d/, /g/) or **voiceless**; the nine voiceless consonants are /p/, /t/, /k/, /f/, /s/, /ʃ/, /h/, /tʃ/ and /θ/.
2. **Place of articulation**: **bilabial** (/p, b, m, w/), **labio-dental** (/f, v/), **inter-dental** (/θ, ð/), **alveolar**, **palato-alveolar** (/ʃ, ʒ/), **palatal** (/j/), **velar** (/k, g, ŋ/) and **glottal** (/h/).
3. **Manner of articulation**: **stops or plosives** (complete closure then sudden release), **fricatives** (narrowing that air forces through), **affricates** (/tʃ, dʒ/), **nasals** (/m, n, ŋ/, air through the nose), **liquids** (/l/ and /r/) and **semi-vowels** (/j, w/).

## Mispronunciation

Nigerian speakers often mispronounce words with **silent letters** (the t in **castle**, the b in **debt**, the h in **honest**) and confuse sounds, such as /θ/ with /t/ (**think**) or /ʃ/ with /tʃ/ (**Chicago**).
