# Prose Fiction

## Definition and origins

**Prose** comes from the Latin **"prosa"**, meaning **"straightforward"**: it is narrative, passed across in a **denotative** form, unlike drama and poetry, which rely on connotation. **Fiction** is an imaginative work: the story or tale of **imaginary characters and events**. It is "untrue" in the absolute sense but **true in the universal sense**, depicting reality. Prose fiction may have evolved from narrative poetry such as **epics** (the *Sundiata* of old Mali) or from **folklore** (myths, legends and folktales, such as tortoise tales); Tutuola's *The Palmwine Drinkard* and Okri's *The Famished Road* draw on folklore.

## Functions

Prose **entertains** (popular fiction, including Nigeria's **Pacesetters Series**), imparts **moral values** and preserves **culture**, and **criticises society**, as Alan Paton's *Cry, the Beloved Country* protested against South Africa's racial structures.

## Fiction and non-fiction

- **Fiction** is imaginative, even when its settings are real.
- **Non-fiction** is true to life: **biography** (a life written by another), **autobiography** (self-writing, such as Mandela's *Long Walk to Freedom*), **memoir** (autobiographical but focused on a specific period, such as Soyinka's *Aké*), **diary** and **letters**.

## Forms by length

- **Flash fiction**: the shortest, from **6 to about 700 words**.
- **Short story**: about **2,000 to 7,500 words**, read at one sitting, such as Jomo Kenyatta's "The Gentlemen of the Jungle".
- **Novella**: at least 17,500 but under 50,000 words, such as Hemingway's ***The Old Man and the Sea*** (1952).
- **Novel**: the longest, from about **50,000 words**, with a main plot and many sub-plots, such as Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958) and Adichie's *Purple Hibiscus*.

## Elements

- **Subject matter**: the summary of events. In Buchi Emecheta's ***Second-Class Citizen*** (a set text), Adah Ofili strives against all odds for education, then migrates to England hoping for greener pastures.
- **Theme**: the central idea or thematic preoccupation.
- **Plot**: **linear or organic** (chronological) or **episodic** (starting in the middle, with flashbacks); its stages are exposition, rising action, **climax**, falling action and **denouement**.
- **Characterisation**: by **naming**, **showing** (the dramatic technique) and **telling**.
- **Narrative point of view**: first-person or third-person omniscient.
- **Setting**: spatial or temporal, real or imaginary.

## Types of character

- the **protagonist** (the central character);
- the **antagonist**, who is in conflict with the protagonist;
- the hero or heroine, and the villain;
- the **eponymous** character, whose name is the title (*Oliver Twist*);
- the **flat** character, two-dimensional and predictable;
- the **round** character, dynamic and changing; and
- the **foil**, who has opposite features to another character.

## Types of novel

Novels include **historical**, **mystery**, **picaresque** (a rogue hero from a low background), **magical realist**, **allegorical** (symbolic, as in *Animal Farm*), **gothic** and **epistolary** (told through letters, as in Mariama Bâ's *So Long a Letter*).
