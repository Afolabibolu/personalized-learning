# The Entrepreneur and Entrepreneurship Development in Nigeria

## The entrepreneur

Entrepreneurship differs from **management**, which focuses on routine administration, and from **self-employment**, which lacks innovation (Drucker, 1985). An entrepreneur is an **innovator**, a **risk taker** and a **developer** who **combines the factors of production (land, labour and capital)** to create products, services and jobs. Their roles are:

- **opportunity seeker and innovator**;
- **risk taker and decision maker**;
- **resource mobiliser**;
- **team leader and builder**;
- **market shaper**; and
- **agent of economic development**.

Their characteristics are **personal traits**, **behavioural and managerial competencies** and **motivational drive**. Drivers include personal drive, **self-efficacy** and an **internal locus of control**, strategic thinking, adaptability and discipline, plus political, economic, social, technological and cultural factors; Nigeria's **Igbo apprenticeship system**, for example, encourages enterprise. The **rewards** are **personal fulfilment**, **wealth creation**, **independence** and **recognition and social impact**.

Case studies include **Aliko Dangote** (industrial expansion and import substitution), **Folorunsho Alakija** (oil and fashion), **Tony Elumelu** (empowerment through the Tony Elumelu Foundation, and the **Africapitalism** vision), Elon Musk, Jack Ma and Anita Roddick.

## The ecosystem

The **entrepreneurial ecosystem** is the interconnected set of social, economic, institutional and cultural elements that influence the creation, survival and growth of ventures. Its components are **policy and regulation, finance, culture, support systems, human capital, and markets and networks**. Examples are **Silicon Valley**, **Shenzhen**, and **Nairobi and Lagos**.

## Nigeria

African entrepreneurship evolved through precolonial trade (**Hausa and Yoruba networks**), colonial disruption, post-independence state control and liberalisation; the **Structural Adjustment Programmes** of the 1980s and 1990s re-empowered private enterprise. Nigerian enterprises are classed as **nano, micro, small, medium and large**. Support agencies include **SMEDAN** and the **Bank of Industry (BOI)**.

**Challenges**: political instability and policy inconsistency, high costs and inflation, **bureaucracy and corruption**, **limited access to finance** (high interest rates and collateral demands), **infrastructure deficits** (erratic power, poor roads), **skills gaps**, limited market access, insecurity, multiple taxation and a cultural preference for paid employment.

**Prospects**: entrepreneurship education in universities, **digitalisation** (e-commerce, fintech), co-working hubs and renewable energy, online business registration and tax incentives, and partnerships among government, academia and industry. Because nano and micro businesses need basic training and credit while growth-oriented ventures need venture capital and incubation, a **dual-policy framework** is recommended.
