# Foundations and Theories of Entrepreneurship

## Meaning

There is **no single, universally accepted definition** of entrepreneurship. The word comes from the French **entreprendre, "to undertake"**. In Africa, entrepreneurship often comes from **necessity as well as opportunity**; in Nigeria, with high unemployment, it is a key route to survival and wealth, and the rise of **Nollywood** shows creativity overcoming limited infrastructure. Modern entrepreneurship goes beyond self-employment to **innovation-driven ventures** that can scale.

## Theorists

- **Richard Cantillon** (1685 to 1734), the "father of economic theory", saw the entrepreneur as a **risk-bearer** who buys at certain prices and sells at uncertain ones.
- **Jean-Baptiste Say** (1767 to 1832) saw the entrepreneur as the **coordinator and primary agent of production**, whose key attribute is **sound judgment**.
- **Joseph Schumpeter** (born 1883) described entrepreneurship as **"creative destruction"**: innovation that disrupts markets and drives development. Innovation can mean a new good, a new method of production, a new market, a new source of raw materials or a new organisation of an industry.
- **Peter Drucker** saw entrepreneurship as purposeful innovation: the entrepreneur **searches for change, responds to it and exploits it as an opportunity**.
- Sociologist **Ronald Burt** argued that one's **network** is a resource that shapes entrepreneurial opportunity.

**Classical** writers (Cantillon, Say) saw entrepreneurs as organisers and risk-bearers; **modern** ones (Schumpeter, Drucker) as **innovators**.

## Myths

Common myths are that entrepreneurs are **born, not made** (skills can be taught), that it is only about trading (it includes **intrapreneurship** and social ventures), that it is only about money, that entrepreneurs take **extreme** risks (they take **calculated** risks) and that **failure is permanent** (it is a learning opportunity).

## Types

**Classical** (small business), **corporate entrepreneurship (intrapreneurship)**, **social entrepreneurship** (impact alongside profit) and **digital entrepreneurship**, such as **Flutterwave**, **Andela**, **Jumia** and Airbnb, which focus on scalability and global reach.

## The entrepreneurial process

The process is a **continuous, non-linear cycle** of identifying, evaluating and exploiting opportunities: **discovery or ideation**, **opportunity evaluation** (market research and a **minimum viable product**), **planning and resourcing**, **launch**, **growth and scaling**, and **harvesting or exit** (sale, IPO or succession).

## Perspectives

Entrepreneurship is studied from **economic** (a catalyst for growth), **sociological** (networks and social change), **psychological** (needs for autonomy and achievement) and **management** (resources and strategy) perspectives. **Opportunity recognition** is the first step, characterised by alertness, information searching, data gathering, communication, problem-solving and evaluation.
