# Indigenous African Political Systems

## Origins of political authority

African societies linked their institutional power with **supernatural elements** found in **myths of origin**. The Zulu believe the High God created chiefs to keep social stability; the Tallensi of Northern Ghana locate their origin in ancestors (tendana); the Nupe anchor theirs on Tsoede. The myths suggest that the state is an offshoot of the supernatural, and political systems were traditionally based on **kinship and lineage**.

## The family

J.F. Ade Ajayi holds that **the family is the unit of political culture** in Africa. In the **nuclear family**, father, mother and children form the core, with a hierarchy of power: the **father** holds executive power as provider, protector and peacemaker, but is not autocratic, and the **mother** occupies the position of a **caretaker** in all aspects of family life, acting as a safety valve and adviser. Their roles are complementary, giving **checks and balances**; there were even female kings, as in Ondo and Ile-Oluji. **Extended families** may be monogamous joint families of several nuclear families, or **polygamous** families of a man, his wives and children. Families, not individuals, are the building blocks of African society, offering responsibility, a collective ethos and solidarity.

## Types of political system

Ajayi identifies about **four types** of political system in traditional African societies:

1. **Centralised** political organisation, with a king at the apex and village heads below.
2. Societies whose rulers held centralised authority over a hierarchy.
3. **Non-centralised (acephalous)** societies with no single ruler, such as the **Igbo** of Nigeria, the Kru of Liberia, the Tallensi of Ghana, the Somali and the Massai. Societies combining both types are called **polycephalous**, like the Kpelle of Liberia, with several paramount chiefs of equal standing.
4. Small **band societies**, whose political organisation was the same as their kinship organisation.

## Kings and councils

Whatever the system, the king was **paramount** and ruled with a group of chiefs, among the Yoruba the **Igbimo** (council of chiefs). He performed executive, legislative and judicial functions, but was **not autocratic in practice**: his **powers and privileges were great but circumscribed**, and he had to earn the people's respect rather than rule by force. Councils of elders could be strongly democratic. Conflict was resolved through these institutions in the interest of social order.
