# Indigenous African Education

## Purpose

Every society transmits its cultural heritage from one generation to another, by its own objectives and methods. In traditional Africa, an **educated man** was "a warrior, a hunter, a nobleman and a man of character", and anyone who combined these with a specific skill was considered well educated and integrated (Fafunwa 1974). According to **Fafunwa, functionalism was the main guiding principle of African education**: education was a means to an end, directed at immediate usefulness, job orientation, political participation and spiritual and moral values.

## Fafunwa's seven objectives

1. To develop the child's latent and **physical** skills.
2. To develop **character**.
3. To inculcate **respect for elders** and those in authority.
4. To develop **intellectual** skills.
5. To acquire **specific vocational training** and develop a healthy attitude towards **honest labour**.
6. To develop a **sense of belonging** and participate in family and community affairs.
7. To understand, appreciate and promote the **cultural heritage** of the community.

**Character training is the primary objective**: a child without good morals is called a child without education. Majasan named character training and religious education as the two major objectives. A Nigerian child is expected to be honest, dutiful, respectful, law-abiding, loyal and sociable, and parents serve as models. Taboos, labour and, later, Islamic education also reinforced character.

## Methods

Learning is acquired mainly through **observation, imitation and participation**: adults demonstrate what they want the child to practise. **Imitating a role model is an indirect method** of learning. Physical training comes from watching adults jump, climb, dance and drum. **Proverbs** enhance the child's **intellectual development**; every child is expected to master and use the proverbs of his or her people. Specific skills were acquired through apprenticeship and through **initiation** by traditional professionals such as herbalists, hunters, chiefs and priests. Secret societies gave a form of higher education through initiation.

## Agents

The **home** is the major agent of socialisation, and the **mother** is the child's **first agent of socialisation**. **Parents are crucial agents of civilisation** in African settings. Peer groups train character through competitive tasks and healthy rivalry, and the wider community, especially elders, teaches history and customs. Many features of traditional education survive despite the import of foreign culture.
