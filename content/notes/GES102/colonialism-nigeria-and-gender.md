# Colonialism, the Evolution of Nigeria and Gender

## Nigeria and colonialism

Nigeria is West Africa's largest country, covering **356,667 square miles**. The name was suggested in **1897** by the British journalist **Flora Shaw**, who later married Frederick Lugard. Its three largest culture groups are the **Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba**.

Before 1900 there were three separate territories under British control: the **Niger Coast Protectorate** (created in 1891 as the Oil Rivers Protectorate), the **Colony of Lagos**, which was **annexed in 1861** (Epe in 1863), and the territory of the **Royal Niger Company**. Nigeria became a colonial territory in **1900**. Conquest was uneven: Ijebuland in 1892, Benin in 1897, and Abeokuta, Tivland and the east not effectively controlled until 1914 to 1916.

In the north, the preacher **Uthman Dan Fodio** criticised the un-Islamic practices of the Hausa rulers and in **1804** declared the **jihad**, which established a theocratic state across Hausaland: a political as well as a religious revolution.

In **1906**, **Sir Walter Egerton** merged the Lagos Colony and Protectorate with the Protectorate of Southern Nigeria. The **amalgamation of the Northern and Southern Protectorates in 1914** first brought the disparate nationalities under one umbrella. Governor **Clifford introduced the elective principle in 1922**, and elected members won on the platform of the Nigerian National Democratic Party. Christian mission schools produced the educated elite of the early twentieth century.

## Gender

**Gender** is a **social construct that apportions roles** to individuals based on sex differentiation: the non-physiological, culturally determined aspects of being male or female. **Sex** is the physiological aspect.

- **Sex roles** are biologically determined and not exchangeable: female sex roles are **child bearing, lactation and gestation**. They are the same in all societies and periods.
- **Gender roles** are the behaviours society defines as appropriate for each sex, such as childcare. They are exchangeable and vary with culture, time and class.

**Gender stereotypes** are expectations about how a person will look, act, think and feel because of his or her sex. They are learned through **socialisation**, the lifelong process of learning society's expectations, by way of the family (toy cars for boys, dolls for girls), school, peer groups, religious institutions and books. **Feminism** is an advocacy for women's rights based on the belief in the equality of the sexes. Its types include liberal, radical, socialist and **Marxist feminism, which brought class analysis** to feminism.
