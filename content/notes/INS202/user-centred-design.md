# User-Centred Design

## User-centred design

**User-centred design (UCD)** builds systems around **real users' needs, evidenced by research**, rather than designers' assumptions. In the lecture's example, an engineer-led team built a hospital booking system that elderly patients could not use, while a UCD team studied patients and nurses first and succeeded. **Evidence beats assumption**: even experienced designers misjudge user needs.

## Design thinking

Design thinking has **five non-linear stages** that loop back as new evidence emerges:

1. **Empathize**: understand users through research.
2. **Define**: state the problem, often as a **"How might we...?"** question.
3. **Ideate**: generate many possible solutions.
4. **Prototype**: build quick versions of ideas.
5. **Test**: try them with users and learn.

## User research

Research methods include **interviews** (in-depth individual views), **surveys** (many responses quickly), **contextual inquiry** (observing and questioning users in their real environment) and **field studies**. The method should fit the design question.

## Personas and journey maps

A **persona** is a **fictional but research-based profile** of a typical user, with goals, frustrations and context, which gives the team a memorable reference point. Personas can oversimplify diverse users, so use several and keep them grounded in data. A **user journey map** shows **what a user experiences over time**, step by step, including **pain points** and emotions.

## Storyboards, wireframes and prototypes

- A **storyboard** is a sequence of panels showing a user, their environment and how they use a product in context.
- A **wireframe** is a **skeletal layout** that shows **structure and content placement** without visual style.
- A **prototype** is a working model for testing, across a **fidelity spectrum** from **low-fidelity** (paper sketches) to **high-fidelity** (fully interactive digital mock-ups).

**Fidelity is a choice, not a ranking**: match it to the project stage and the question being tested. Paper prototypes are fast and cheap and invite honest criticism early.

## Iterative design

**Iterative design** repeats a **design, test and refine** loop, each cycle targeting specific, evidence-based problems. In the lecture's example, a **login screen's success rate rose from 60% to over 95% across three cycles**. **User feedback** drives each iteration. UCD is a **pipeline, not a checklist**: each stage's output feeds the next, as in the worked library kiosk example, and skipping a stage, such as testing, breaks the chain.
