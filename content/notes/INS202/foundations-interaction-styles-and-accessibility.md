# Foundations: Interaction Styles, Diversity and Accessibility

## Six disciplines

HCI draws on **computer science, psychology, design, ergonomics, sociology and linguistics**. **Sociology** studies how groups behave, which matters for social media and collaborative tools. **Linguistics** shapes menu wording, chatbots and error messages: "Invalid input in field 7" is poor; "Please enter your date of birth as DD/MM/YYYY" is good. A common mistake is writing interface text the way a programmer thinks rather than the way a user thinks.

## Human and computer factors

- **Human factors**: **physical** (vision, hearing, dexterity, reach), **cognitive** (attention, memory, learning) and **social** (culture, prior experience). An elderly patient is better served by a kiosk with **large, well-spaced buttons**. Do not design for yourself; **involve real users early**.
- **Computer factors**: **input devices** (keyboards, touchscreens, microphones, sensors), **output devices** (screens, speakers, printers, **haptic** feedback), **processing** (response speed affects perceived usability) and **dialogue style** (one question at a time in a wizard, or everything at once in a dashboard). For a surgeon needing sterile, hands-free control, **voice or gesture** is best.

## Six interaction styles

| Style | Learning curve | Expert speed | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Command line | Steep | Very fast | Terminal |
| Menu-driven | Low | Moderate | ATM menus |
| Form-fill | Low | Moderate | Visa application |
| Direct manipulation | Low to moderate | Fast | Dragging files, pinch-to-zoom |
| Natural language | Very low | Variable | Voice assistants, chatbots |
| Gesture | Low to moderate | Fast | Swipes, motion games |

**Novice-facing** systems favour menus and forms; **expert** tools favour command lines and shortcuts. An **ATM** uses menus so first-time customers need no training; **Excel** mixes direct manipulation, menus and a command-like formula bar.

## User diversity

There is **no "average user"**. Users differ in **expertise, age, culture** (a thumbs-up means approval in most Western cultures), **language** (true localisation, not literal translation), **disability** and **technical literacy**. A farming app for smallholders should use icons, voice prompts and local languages.

## Accessibility and inclusive design

**Accessibility** is designing systems that people with **visual, auditory, motor or cognitive disabilities** can perceive, understand, navigate and use (W3C WAI). The **WCAG** principles are **POUR**:

- **Perceivable**: text alternatives for images, contrast and captions;
- **Operable**: full keyboard navigation and enough time;
- **Understandable**: clear, consistent navigation; and
- **Robust**: compatible with **assistive technologies** such as screen readers, magnifiers and switch access.

An unlabelled "Submit" icon blocks a screen-reader user. **Inclusive design** makes products usable by as many people as possible from the outset, including **temporary** (a broken arm) and **situational** (holding a baby) limitations. **Accessibility is a subset of inclusive design.** The **curb-cut effect** shows that solutions for disability often help everyone.
