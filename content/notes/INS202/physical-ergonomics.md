# Physical Ergonomics

## Why ergonomics matters

Two workers can do the same job: one with an **adjustable chair, monitor at eye level and straight wrists**, the other hunched over a laptop on their knees at a kitchen table. Over months, the second faces far more strain. **Physical ergonomics** is the **physical fit between humans and machines**, and it treats the **desk, chair, monitor, keyboard and mouse as one system**: fixing one element alone rarely solves the problem.

## Workspace design

**ISO 9241-5** gives five core principles: **neutral posture**, **adjustability**, **clearance**, **sightlines** and **reach zones**. The monitor should be **at or slightly below eye level, 50 to 70 cm away**, and frequently used items should sit within easy reach. The **"90-90-90 rule"** (elbows, knees and hips at about 90°, feet flat) is a **starting heuristic, not a rigid law**. A typing desk is about 68 to 75 cm high, with forearms parallel to the floor.

## Input devices

**Keyboard and mouse design** affect wrist and forearm strain through key travel, layout, grip and shape. Ergonomic alternatives, such as split keyboards and vertical mice, **trade familiarity for reduced risk**. **Mobile ergonomics** introduces **thumb-zone design**, placing key controls where the thumb reaches easily in **one-handed** use, now standard in major apps.

## Displays

**Resolution, refresh rate, brightness and colour accuracy** affect comfort and, in fields such as radiology and aviation, **task accuracy and safety**. Glare and poor contrast add strain.

## Eye strain and RSI

**Digital eye strain** comes from long screen use: tired, dry eyes, blurred vision and headaches. **Repetitive strain injury (RSI)**, including **carpal tunnel syndrome**, comes from **cumulative, everyday** repeated movements and awkward postures. The key preventive practices are:

- the **20-20-20 rule**: **every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds**; and
- regular **micro-breaks**, stretching and good device placement.

## Posture and responsibility

**No single "correct" posture exists: postural variation beats any one ideal.** Neither perfect sitting nor a standing desk alone is the answer; alternating positions matters most. **User feedback and ergonomic assessments**, such as workstation audits, find problems early. **Occupational health is a shared responsibility**: organisations must assess, adjust and train, because individual willpower alone cannot fix systemic hazards, while designers create safer products and individuals take breaks and adjust their setups.
