# Electric Field and Gauss's Law

## The electric field

The **electric field** at a point is the force per unit positive test charge: **E = F/q₀**, measured in **N/C (= V/m)**. A charge q in a field therefore feels **F = qE**. That force is **parallel** to E for positive charge and opposite for negative.

The field of a **point charge** is **E = kq/r²**, radially outward for positive q and inward for negative q. Fields from several charges add as vectors.

**Field lines**:

- start on positive charges and end on negative ones;
- never cross;
- are closer together where the field is stronger;
- are perpendicular to conductor surfaces.

The field always points in the direction of **decreasing potential**.

## Uniform fields

Between parallel plates with potential difference V and separation d, the field is uniform: **E = V/d**.

An electron released there accelerates at a = eE/m. With V = 25 V and d = 0.5 m, E = 50 V/m, so a = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 50/(9.1 × 10⁻³¹) ≈ **8.79 × 10¹² m/s²**. Its path in a transverse field is a parabola, as in a cathode-ray tube.

## Gauss's law

The **electric flux** through a surface is Φ = ∮E·dA. **Gauss's law**: the total flux through any **closed** surface equals the enclosed charge divided by ε₀:

**∮E·dA = q_enc/ε₀**.

It gives fields of symmetric distributions quickly:

- point charge: E = q/(4πε₀r²);
- infinite line of charge density λ: E = λ/(2πε₀r);
- infinite sheet of charge density σ: **E = σ/(2ε₀)**;
- just outside a conductor: E = σ/ε₀.

Inside a conductor in equilibrium, **E = 0** and any excess charge sits on the surface.

Fields are produced by charges, whether at rest or moving. Static fields do not need motion, so "fields are caused by the motion of charges" is false for electric fields: moving charges produce *magnetic* fields.
