# Research Ideation and the Research Problem

## Planning research

Research planning has four steps: orient yourself to knowledge creation, **define the research question**, **review previous research** on it, and **select and analyse data** to answer it. Planning is iterative, and the question is refined many times.

## Sources of research ideas

- **Previous research activities**, including the "recommendations for future work" sections of papers.
- **Article readings**, in three phases: **surface reading**, **technical reading** (stop where you lose the picture and clear it up) and **guided reading** with a supervisor. Keep copies and write a summary of each paper (title, authors, aim, methodology, results, strengths and weaknesses); this builds Chapter Two.
- **Observations**, informal or formal, such as a parking problem in the faculty.
- **Experience** from one's line of work.
- **Discussions and colloquia.**

When choosing a topic, stay within the assignment's guidelines, pick something of personal interest, make sure there is a manageable amount of information, be original, and state the topic as a question.

## The research problem

**Identification** of the problem, not its selection, is the first step in research. A **research problem** is a clear and definite statement about an area of concern, a difficulty to eliminate, a condition to improve or a troubling problem in theory, literature or practice. It matters because it **sets the scope** and ties the work to goals and actions.

**Characteristics:** a good research problem is **specific**, **measurable**, **achievable**, **realistic** and **time bound**.

**Elements:** **why** (the reason for the study), **what** (what is investigated), **where**, **when** (the period of study) and **who** (from whom data is collected).

**Identification steps:** choose the field, master it, review previous research, set priorities, draw insight or use experience (with a supervisor's help), and pin-point the specific aspect to investigate.

**Sources:** personal and practical experience, literature, previous research, existing theories, social issues, brainstorming, experts, the community, a questioning attitude, and the supervisor.

## Formulation

1. Identify a broad field of interest.
2. Dissect it into sub-areas.
3. Select the one of most interest.
4. Raise research questions.
5. Formulate objectives.
6. Assess the objectives.
7. Double check.

**Common mistakes:** not saying why the problem matters, weak structuring, insufficiently motivated questions, unresearchable problems, favouring a method, and blind data mining.

## Statement of the problem

Kerlinger (1973) gives three criteria for a good problem statement: it should concern the **relation between two or more variables**, be stated **clearly and unambiguously in question form**, and be **amenable to empirical testing**.
