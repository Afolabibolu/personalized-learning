# Professional Issues in Computer Science

## What a profession is

A **profession** is an occupation in which one professes extensive knowledge, acquired through long experience and formal education, together with the **autonomy** and responsibility to make independent decisions. Law, medicine and engineering are well known professions. In the Middle Ages, a profession meant a commitment professed to join a religious order.

## Requirements of a professional

1. **Highly developed skills and deep knowledge** of the domain, backed by long years of formal schooling.
2. **Autonomy:** in the provider–client relationship the power balance favours the professional, as with a lawyer and client or a physician and patient.
3. **Observance of a code of conduct.** A professional observes four codes:
   - the **professional code**, set by the profession;
   - a **personal code**, from culture and religion;
   - the **institutional code** of the employer;
   - the **community code**, from local religion or culture.

The codes are like concentric circles, with the community code at the centre, then the institutional, personal and professional codes. For an action to be ethical, moral and legal it must conform to all four, which can create **moral dilemmas**, as for a doctor asked to assist euthanasia where neither hospital nor community supports it.

## The four pillars of professionalism

- **Commitment:** made willingly, pursued even if help is needed, with agreement on what is done, by whom and when, stated openly, not made easily, and renegotiated with advance notice if it cannot be met.
- **Integrity:** undivided loyalty to one's beliefs, from the Latin *integratas* (whole); its three maxims are **vision**, **love** of what one does and **commitment**.
- **Responsibility:** roles, tasks and actions and their consequences. As a provider, a professional has **service** responsibilities (time and quality of delivery), **product** responsibilities (delivery on time, quality, documentation and liability) and **consequential** responsibilities (safety of the client from physical and financial harm).
- **Accountability:** the obligation to answer for one's responsibilities, with **outcome measures**, **performance standards** and **incentives or penalties**.

## Enforcement

When a member is accused of misconduct, the profession holds a **hearing**. If the member is found guilty, **sanctions** may include probation, revocation of certification, a request to resign or suspension. A profession must also have an **appeals** process with clear procedures and time limits.

Other professional issues include the process of **ethical decision making**, the guilt felt about wrong decisions, **whistle-blowing**, and social issues such as **harassment** and **discrimination**.
