# Economics and Development

## Origins and subject matter

Economics grew out of moral philosophy, law and religion. It became a distinct discipline with Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776). Its subject matter is **scarcity**: human wants are unlimited, but the resources to satisfy them are limited, so people must **choose**. Economic theory assumes **rationality** in decisions, although family, culture and peers also influence them. Choosing one want means giving up another, and the next-best alternative given up is the **opportunity cost**.

## Production, consumption and exchange

- **Production** creates goods and services. It answers what to produce, how much, how (the mix of capital and labour), and how to organise it.
- **Consumption** "uses up" goods and services. It can be intermediate (for further production) or final; durable, such as cars, or non-durable, such as food; and a necessity or a luxury.
- **Distribution and exchange** connect production and consumption, which are separated in place and time. For example, recharge cards are made in a few cities but used everywhere, and crops are harvested seasonally but eaten all year.

Producers respond to demand. A temporary rise in demand is met by using more of the installed capacity; a permanent rise needs new capacity, through capital formation.

The **law of demand** says that the higher the price, the lower the quantity demanded. Changes in the good's own price move along the demand curve. Changes in the prices of related goods, income, taxes or tastes shift the curve.

## Definitions of economics

- **Adam Smith:** "an inquiry into the nature and causes of the wealth of nations".
- **J.S. Mill:** "the practical science of the production and distribution of wealth".
- **Alfred Marshall:** "the study of mankind in the ordinary business of life".
- **L. Robbins:** a social science that studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means that have alternative uses. This is one of the most accepted definitions.

Economics is a **science** because it uses systematic methods and **models**, simplified representations of reality, to generalise about economic relationships. Its scope now includes institutions and environmental effects such as climate change. It also has applied branches: agricultural, health, monetary and environmental economics.

## Growth and development

**Economic growth** is "a steady process by which the productive capacity of the economy is increased over time to bring about rising levels of national income" (Todaro 2015). **Economic development** is far more than growth. Traditionally it meant sustaining annual output growth of 5% or more. It has since been redefined as **reducing poverty, inequality and unemployment** within a growing economy: the sustained elevation of a whole society towards a better life.

Growth does not necessarily bring development, but development presupposes growth, so growth is a subset of development. Development is driven by a nation's **stock of capital**. Low capital accumulation in developing countries keeps productivity low and poverty high.
