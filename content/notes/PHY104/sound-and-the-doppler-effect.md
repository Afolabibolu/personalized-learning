# Sound and the Doppler Effect

## Sound

Sound is a **longitudinal** mechanical wave of compressions and rarefactions, and it cannot travel in a vacuum. Its speed is:

- **√(Y/ρ)** in a solid;
- **√(B/ρ)** in a fluid;
- **√(γP/ρ) = √(γRT/M)** in a gas, so it rises with temperature (about 340 m/s in air at room temperature).

**Intensity** is power per unit area, falling as 1/r² from a point source. The intensity level is **β = 10 log(I/I₀) dB**, with I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m². Pitch depends on frequency, loudness on intensity, and quality (timbre) on the overtones present.

## The Doppler effect

When source and observer move relative to the medium, the observed frequency is

**f' = f (v + v₀)/(v − vₛ)**,

with velocities taken as positive when the observer moves **towards** the source and the source moves **towards** the observer.

- **Approaching** raises the pitch; **receding** lowers it.
- If both move the same way at the same speed, there is no relative approach and **f' = f**. If their speeds differ, f' can be higher, lower or equal.

**Example**: a car's 440 Hz horn heard as 415 Hz by a cyclist following at 30 m/s (v = 343 m/s). The cyclist approaches the source and the car recedes, so 415 = 440(343 + 30)/(343 + vₛ). That gives vₛ = 52.5 m/s = **189 km/h**.

**Example**: two 500 Hz whistles. P is stationary and Q moves away at 6 m/s; an observer between them moves towards Q at 2 m/s.

- From P: f = 500(340 − 2)/340 = 497.1 Hz.
- From Q: f = 500(340 + 2)/(340 + 6) = 494.2 Hz.
- The beat frequency is about **3 beats/s**.

## Shock waves

When the source moves at or faster than sound, the wavefronts pile up into a **bow (shock) wave**: the **sonic boom**. At vₛ = v the formula fails for observers **in front**, because the denominator becomes zero. The half-angle of the shock cone is given by sin θ = v/vₛ, where vₛ/v is the **Mach number**.

Uses of the Doppler effect include police radar, blood-flow meters and finding the speeds of stars (red shift).
