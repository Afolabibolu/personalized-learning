# Atomic Structure and Quantum Effects

## The atom

An atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** containing **protons** (+e, 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) and **neutrons** (no charge, 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg). It is surrounded by **electrons** (−e, 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg) in an electron cloud that fills most of the atom's volume. Dalton (1803) proposed atoms from the laws of chemical combination. (The slide's particle table swaps the charges of the proton and neutron; the values here are correct.)

## Atomic models

- **Thomson**: positive charge with embedded electrons.
- **Rutherford**: nuclear model from α-scattering.
- **Bohr**: electrons in fixed orbits with **quantised energies**. It explains the **line spectrum of hydrogen**.
- The **mechanical model** (atoms as hard spheres) explains the laws of chemical combination and the kinetic theory of gases, but **not** chemical (electrovalent) bonding.

**Elementary particles** are classed by spin. **Fermions** have half-integer spin and obey the Pauli exclusion principle (electrons, protons, neutrinos). **Bosons** have integer spin (photons, pions).

## Energy levels

In hydrogen, **Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV**, so the **ionisation energy** is 13.6 eV. An atom excited from E₀ to E₂, for example by collision, gains energy that the colliding atom **loses**. On returning it emits a photon with **hf = E₂ − E₀**. Each transition gives one spectral line.

## Cathode rays and the photoelectric effect

**Cathode rays** are streams of electrons, produced by **thermionic emission** from a hot cathode or by a discharge. They are deflected by electric and magnetic fields.

**Photoelectric effect**: light above a **threshold frequency** ejects electrons from a metal at once. **Einstein's equation** is **hf = φ + ½mv²_max**, where φ is the work function. Intensity changes the *number* of electrons, not their energy; this shows that light comes in photons.

**Example**: a photon of 3.8 × 10¹⁵ Hz carries 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.8 × 10¹⁵/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 15.7 eV. Ionising hydrogen leaves the electron with **15.7 − 13.6 ≈ 2.1 eV**.

## X-rays

X-rays are produced when **fast electrons strike a heavy-metal target** in an X-ray tube. Most of the energy becomes heat, so the target is cooled. The shortest wavelength is **λ_min = hc/(eV)**. X-rays are very penetrating and ionising: they are used in medical imaging, radiotherapy and crystal analysis.
