# Variables, Data and Sampling

## Variables, populations and samples

A **variable** is a **characteristic or condition that can change or take different values**, such as class size or academic performance. Research usually asks how two variables are related for a group. The entire group of interest is the **population**; because populations are usually too large to study, a **sample** is selected to **represent** it, and results from the sample are used to answer questions about the population.

A **statistic** is a **characteristic of a sample** (such as the sample mean), while a **parameter** is a **characteristic of a population** (such as the population mean μ). Statistics are used to estimate parameters.

## Types of variable

- **Discrete** variables consist of **indivisible categories or counts**, such as class size or the number of children.
- **Continuous** variables, such as time or weight, are **infinitely divisible**: time can be measured to the nearest minute, second or half-second.

Because a continuous measurement is never exact, each value covers an interval bounded by its **real limits**, located **exactly halfway between adjacent categories**: a recorded weight of 150 kg has real limits 149.5 and 150.5.

## Scales of measurement

- **Nominal**: categories with **no meaningful order**, such as favourite subject or gender.
- **Ordinal**: categories with an **obvious order**, such as strongly disagree, disagree, not sure, agree, strongly agree.
- **Interval** and **ratio (scale)**: numerical measurements with equal units, such as an exam score from 0% to 100%; ratio scales have a true zero.

Nominal and ordinal data are **categorical** (qualitative); interval and ratio data are **scale** (quantitative). Discrete and continuous variables are two kinds of **quantitative classification**.

## Sampling

**Random (probability) sampling** gives every member a known chance of selection:

- **simple random sampling**, where every sample of size n is equally likely;
- **systematic sampling**, taking every kth member;
- **stratified sampling**, sampling separately within groups (strata); and
- **cluster sampling**, selecting whole groups.

**Non-random sampling** is cheaper but risks bias: **convenience sampling** (whoever is easiest to reach), **judgement (purposive) sampling** (chosen by the researcher), and **quota sampling**, a stratified form in which a set number is gathered from each group **on a convenience basis**.

**Randomly assigning treatments** to experimental units allows **causal inference**, while **randomly sampling** from a population allows **inference to that population**.
