# Philosophical Problems and Basic Concepts

## Perennial problems

Philosophical problems arise when principles, beliefs or ideas **conflict**, so the same facts are interpreted in inconsistent ways. They are **perennial (recurrent)** because they have **no generally accepted solutions**.

## Epistemological problems

Epistemology distinguishes **empirical knowledge** (from experience) from **a priori knowledge** (from reason alone). Its greatest challenge is **scepticism**, the claim that knowledge is impossible or that we are never justified in claiming certainty. For the rationalist **Plato**, the senses cannot give reliable knowledge; for the empiricist **David Hume**, reason alone cannot. Illusion and hallucination are major obstacles.

- **Philosophical scepticism**: since we cannot rule out error, we cannot know the external world.
- **Methodological scepticism**, held by **René Descartes**: doubt everything to arrive at what **cannot be doubted**.
- **Global scepticism**: we cannot know anything at all.
- **Local scepticism**: knowledge is unavailable in a particular area.

**Feeling certain is not the same as knowing**: we can feel certain and be wrong. We have knowledge when we have a **relevantly justified true belief**.

## Metaphysical problems

- **Ontology** asks what exists. **Nihilism** holds that nothing really exists, **materialism** that **only matter** exists, and **idealism** that everything exists **in the mind** of humans or God.
- **The problem of universals**: **particulars** (a single blue ball) can be in only one place at a time, while **universals** (blueness) can be wholly present in many places. **Realists** hold that universals exist objectively; **nominalists** hold they are only **names** for resembling particulars.
- **The mind-body problem**: **dualism** holds that a person is two entities, a material body and an immaterial mind. **Monism** holds that a person is one entity. **Descartes**, a dualist and **interactionist**, held that mind and body interact through the **pineal gland**.

## Basic concepts

A **concept** is **an idea, notion or understanding of something**. Concepts are basically abstract, though some refer to physical objects. There are four broad types:

1. **Concrete** concepts refer to whole objects (house, car); **abstract** concepts refer to features in isolation (whiteness, justice, honesty).
2. **Relative** concepts presuppose another object (parent and child, husband and wife); **non-relative** concepts stand alone (table, village).
3. **Positive** concepts affirm a quality (a literate person); **negative** concepts show its absence (an illiterate person).
4. **Collective** concepts refer to a group as one whole (team, flock, herd); the whole cannot be attributed to each member, since "a tree is a forest" is absurd. **Non-collective** concepts apply to each member (river, house).
