# Informal Fallacies and the Uses of Language

## Informal fallacies

**Informal fallacies** are errors in reasoning we fall into through **carelessness or inattention**, or because we want to **trick others** into accepting a position. They divide into **fallacies of relevance** (the premises are not related to the conclusion) and **fallacies of ambiguity** (words with more than one meaning).

- **Argumentum ad baculum (appeal to force)**: using force or the threat of force to win acceptance, epitomised by **"might is right"**.
- **Argumentum ad hominem (abusive)**, "argument directed to the man": **attacking the person** instead of their claim, as in "Jane will be a bad journalist because she had a failed love affair".
- **Ad hominem (circumstantial)**: appealing to the opponent's **circumstances**, as when a hunter accused of cruelty replies, "Why do you feed on the flesh of harmless cattle?"
- **Argumentum ad ignorantiam (argument from ignorance)**: claiming something is **true because it has not been proved false**, or false because it has not been proved true, as in "There must be ghosts because no one has proved there are none".
- **Argumentum ad misericordiam (appeal to pity)**: appealing to pity when the question is one of **fact**, as when a youth who killed his parents pleads for leniency because he is an orphan.
- **Argumentum ad populum (appeal to the people)**: an **emotional appeal "to the gallery"**. Popular acceptance does not prove a claim true or a policy wise.
- **Argumentum ad verecundiam (appeal to authority)**: citing an authority on a matter **outside their field**.
- **Accident**: applying a **general rule to a special case** where it does not fit, such as insisting a promise must be kept by returning a gun to a man who has become insane.
- **Converse accident (hasty generalisation)**: **generalising from insufficient evidence**.

## Meaning and the uses of language

Careful and correct use of language is a powerful aid to **straight thinking**. **Language** is a system of signs and symbols that stand for something external to them and make communication possible. **Copi** identifies **three basic functions** of language:

- the **informative** function, communicating information (true or false) by affirming or denying **propositions**;
- the **expressive** function, conveying **feelings, attitudes and emotions** ("Ouch!"), which **cannot be true or false**; and
- the **directive** function, causing or preventing action, as in commands and requests.

Words also have **meaning** in two senses: the **extensional meaning (denotation)** of a word is the class of things it refers to (the extension of "president" includes Obasanjo), while its **intensional meaning (connotation)** is the set of properties those things share. **Definitions** clarify meaning and remove the ambiguity that breeds fallacies.
