# Categorical Syllogisms and Formal Fallacies

## Fallacies

A **fallacy** is **any sort of mistake in reasoning or inference**: an argument that **may seem correct but proves, on examination, not to be so** (Copi). A fallacious argument has premises irrelevant to its conclusion yet appears **psychologically persuasive**. Detecting fallacies ensures clear thinking. Fallacies are **formal** or **informal**.

**Formal fallacies** are **violations of the rules of valid inference**; they occur in formal logic, which deals with **deductive** arguments. Because validity is formal, any argument that breaks a rule of valid inference is fallacious, whatever its content. Affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are formal fallacies.

## The categorical syllogism

A **syllogism** is an argument in which a conclusion is inferred **from two premises**. A **categorical syllogism** infers a categorical conclusion from two categorical propositions and has **exactly three terms**:

- the **major term**, the **predicate of the conclusion**;
- the **minor term**, the **subject of the conclusion**; and
- the **middle term**, which appears in **both premises but not in the conclusion**.

In "No heroes are cowards. Some soldiers are cowards. Therefore, some soldiers are not heroes", "cowards" is the middle term, "soldiers" the minor and "heroes" the major term. In **standard form**, the **major premise** (containing the major term) comes first, the **minor premise** second and the conclusion last; an argument out of order should be rearranged, starting by locating its conclusion.

Validity depends on **form, not content**. An invalid argument can be exposed by **refutation by logical analogy**: constructing another argument with the same form and obviously true premises but a false conclusion. "All robots are fast runners. All heroes are fast runners. Therefore, all heroes are robots" has the same form as "All dogs are mammals. All cats are mammals. Therefore, all cats are dogs".

## Rules and their fallacies

1. A valid syllogism must have **only three terms, each used in the same sense**. Four terms commit the **fallacy of four terms (quaternio terminorum)**; one term used in two senses commits **equivocation**.
2. **The middle term must be distributed in at least one premise.** A term is **distributed** if it refers to **every member** of its class. "All traditionalists are conservatives. Some scholars are conservatives. Therefore, some scholars are traditionalists" commits the **fallacy of the undistributed middle**.
3. **No term may be distributed in the conclusion unless it is distributed in a premise.** "All dogs are mammals. No cats are dogs. Therefore, no cats are mammals" commits the **illicit major** (illicit process of the major term); the same error with the minor term is the **illicit minor**.

Further rules concern negative premises: two negative premises yield no valid conclusion, and a negative premise requires a negative conclusion.
