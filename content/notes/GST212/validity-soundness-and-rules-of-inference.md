# Validity, Soundness and Rules of Inference

## Truth, validity and soundness

**Truth and falsehood belong to statements (propositions), not arguments**, while **validity and invalidity belong only to deductive arguments**. A deductive argument is **valid** if its **premises logically imply the conclusion**: it is **impossible to accept the premises and deny the conclusion without self-contradiction**. Validity is **formal**: it depends on the argument's **form**, not on the truth of its premises, so **a valid argument can have false premises and a false conclusion**. Any argument with a valid form is valid, and any with an invalid form is invalid.

An argument is **sound** if it is **valid and its premises are true**, so its conclusion is true too. **An argument cannot be sound unless it is valid.** For example: "All goats are mammals. All mammals have lungs. Therefore, all goats have lungs."

## Conditionals

A conditional statement, "If p, then q", has an **antecedent** (the part after "if") and a **consequent** (the part after "then"). In "If John passes the course, then I will be surprised", "John passes the course" is the antecedent.

## Valid argument forms

1. **Modus ponens (MP)**: If p then q; p; therefore q. "If it rains, the ground will be wet. It rains. Therefore, the ground is wet."
2. **Modus tollens (MT)**: If p then q; not q; therefore not p. "The ground is not wet. Therefore, it did not rain."
3. **Hypothetical syllogism (HS)**: If p then q; if q then r; therefore if p then r.
4. **Disjunctive syllogism (DS)**: Either p or q; not p; therefore q. "Either Olu visits Ibadan or Kano. He did not visit Ibadan. Therefore, he visited Kano."
5. **Constructive dilemma (CD)**: (If p then q) and (if r then s); p or r; therefore q or s.
6. **Destructive dilemma (DD)**: (If p then q) and (if r then s); not q or not s; therefore not p or not r.

## Invalid look-alikes

- **Affirming the consequent**: "If it rains, the ground will be wet. The ground is wet. Therefore, it rained." This is invalid, because the ground could be wet for another reason.
- **Denying the antecedent**: "If it rains, the ground will be wet. It did not rain. Therefore, the ground is not wet." This is also invalid.

Other valid rules of propositional logic include **conjunction** (the truth of two propositions implies the truth of their conjunction), **simplification** and **addition**. The **truth table method** tests validity mechanically.
