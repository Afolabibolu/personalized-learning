# The Nature of Logic and Arguments

## What logic is

Logic is often called "the science of the laws of thought", but that is too vague, since **psychology** also studies thought. A better definition: logic is **the study of the laws of reasoning**. Reasoning involves making **inferences**, reaching conclusions from premises, and logic's main concern is to **distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning**. It helps us avoid errors in reasoning, called **fallacies**, and gives techniques for testing arguments.

## The laws of thought

1. **Law of identity**: **if any statement is true, then it is true** (p ⊃ p, a tautology).
2. **Law of contradiction (non-contradiction)**: **no statement can be both true and false** (p · ~p is always false).
3. **Law of excluded middle**: **every statement is either true or false** (p ∨ ~p).

## Informal and formal logic

- **Informal logic** deals with our everyday making and evaluating of claims and assesses arguments in **natural, ordinary language**, as in discussions and debates. Its topics include the nature, identification and kinds of arguments, and fallacies.
- **Formal logic** deals with the **logical form or structure** of statements and arguments, without considering their content; it is "the science of pure forms". **Propositional logic** studies compound statements formed with the connectives **and** (·), **or** (∨), **if...then** (⊃), **if and only if** (≡) and **not** (~).

## Arguments

An **argument** is made up of statements, at least one **premise** and at least one **conclusion**, related so that the premises **support** the conclusion. The conclusion is the main claim; the premises are the reasons. **There is no fixed order**: the conclusion may come first, last or between the premises. In **standard form**, premises come first and the conclusion is marked with "therefore" (∴).

- **Premise indicators**: since, because, for, as, in view of, the reason is that, may be inferred from.
- **Conclusion indicators**: therefore, hence, thus, so, consequently, it follows that.

## Deductive and inductive arguments

- In a **deductive argument**, the premises give **conclusive (100%) support**: **if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false**, and all the information in the conclusion is contained in the premises. It typically moves from universal premises to a particular conclusion: "All humans are mortal. Awolowo is human. Therefore, Awolowo is mortal."
- In an **inductive argument**, the premises give only **probable** support, and the conclusion **goes beyond** the premises. It typically moves from particular cases to a general conclusion: "Awolowo, Azikiwe and Ojukwu were human and mortal; therefore, probably all humans are mortal." A single counter-example, such as a white eaglet, can overturn an inductive generalisation that all eaglets are black.
