# Requirements Gathering and Feasibility Study

## Why it matters

Industry surveys show that many IT project failures trace back to **poorly understood requirements and inadequate feasibility assessment**, not programming or hardware faults. **Requirement gathering defines what should be built; the feasibility study confirms whether it should be built at all.**

## Kinds of requirement

- **Functional requirements** describe **what the system does**: the behaviours, functions and services it must perform, its inputs, processing and outputs. They are usually written as "**The system shall...**" and are the easiest for stakeholders to state.
- **Non-functional requirements** (performance or quality-of-service requirements) describe **how well** it works: **performance, usability, availability, reliability**, security, supportability, testability and portability.
- Requirements are also classed as **business**, **user** and **system** requirements.

## The requirements engineering process

Its activities are **elicitation**, **analysis**, **specification**, **validation** and **management**. Requirements are prioritised, for example with **MoSCoW**: **Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time**. The **systems analyst** works with stakeholders such as users, managers, sponsors and developers.

## Fact-finding techniques

1. **Interviews**: planned, face-to-face or virtual conversations with prepared questions, giving **in-depth** information.
2. **Questionnaires**: mostly **closed-ended** (multiple choice, rating scales) with a few open-ended questions; they reach **large, dispersed** groups cheaply, give quantifiable data and allow **anonymous**, candid answers.
3. **Observation**: watching users in their real work.
4. **Document analysis**: studying forms, reports, manuals and records.
5. **Brainstorming**: group generation of ideas and requirements.

## Feasibility

1. **Technical feasibility**: does the organisation have, or can it obtain, the **hardware, software and people** to build and support the system?
2. **Economic feasibility**: do the lifetime **benefits exceed the lifetime costs**? It uses **cost-benefit analysis**.
3. **Operational feasibility**: will the system **work in day-to-day operations**, and will users accept and use it?
4. **Legal feasibility**: does it comply with **laws, regulations, contracts and standards**, such as data protection and intellectual property?
5. **Schedule feasibility**: can it be delivered **within the required time frame**?

## Cost-benefit analysis and risk

**Cost-benefit analysis** compares anticipated costs with anticipated benefits throughout the SDLC. Its common methods are **payback analysis**, **return on investment (ROI)** and **present value (NPV)** analysis. Costs and benefits are **tangible** (measurable in money, such as hardware, licences and salaries) or **intangible** (real but hard to quantify, such as staff morale during transition), and direct or indirect. A **risk register** lists major project risks and mitigations. The feasibility report ends with a recommendation: **proceed, proceed with modification, defer or abandon**.
