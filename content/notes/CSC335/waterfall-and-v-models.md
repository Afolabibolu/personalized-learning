# The Waterfall and V Models

## The waterfall model

The **waterfall model** was one of the first process models. It breaks project activities into **linear sequential phases**: each phase depends on the deliverables of the previous one and is carried out completely before the next begins, with no backing up or repeating. Each major phase is marked by **milestones and deliverables**, and the project manager plans at length in advance.

**When to use it:**

- requirements are very well documented, clear and fixed;
- the product definition is stable;
- the technology is understood and not dynamic;
- there are no ambiguous requirements;
- ample skilled resources are available;
- the project is short.

**Advantages:**

- simple and easy to understand and explain to customers;
- highly structured, so good for beginners;
- little customer involvement needed after specification;
- easy to manage, with specific deliverables and reviews per phase;
- clearly defined stages and milestones;
- well documented;
- allows departmentalisation and control.

**Disadvantages:**

- it does not allow much reflection or revision; once in testing, it is very hard to go back;
- inflexible to changing requirements;
- no working software until late in the life cycle;
- high risk and uncertainty;
- poor for complex, object-oriented, long or ongoing projects;
- progress is hard to measure within stages;
- integration is a "big bang" at the end.

## Waterfall with prototyping

A **prototype** is a partially developed product. Adding prototyping to the waterfall lets developers assess alternative designs (a **design prototype**) and lets users see what the system will be like (a **user-interface prototype**), which helps verification and validation.

## The V model

The **V model** extends the waterfall. Instead of moving down in a straight line, the steps bend upward after coding to form a V. It shows the relationship between each development phase and its associated testing phase. It is also called the **Verification and Validation model**: verification phases (requirements, system design, architecture, module design) run down the left side, validation phases (unit, integration, system and acceptance testing) run up the right, and **coding joins the two sides**. The testing for each phase is planned in parallel with that phase.

It is used where requirements are clear and fixed, the product definition is stable, the technology is well understood and the project is short. It is common in the disciplined **medical** field.

- **Merits:** very easy to understand, apply and manage; highly disciplined; phases completed one at a time; good for small, well-understood projects.
- **Demerits:** inflexible and expensive to change; high risk and uncertainty; poor for complex, long or object-oriented projects; hard to change functionality once in testing; no working software until late.
