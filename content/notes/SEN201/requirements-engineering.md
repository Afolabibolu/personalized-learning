# Requirements Engineering

## What and why

**Requirements engineering** is the process of **discovering, analysing, documenting and maintaining the requirements** of a software system. It answers **"What should the system do?"** and **"What constraints must it satisfy?"**. It ensures the system meets users' needs, improves quality and prevents misunderstandings between customers and developers. **Poor requirements lead to project failure, increased cost, delays in delivery and unsatisfied users.** A **stakeholder** is anyone with an interest in the system: customers, system administrators, government regulators and end users.

## Activities

The four main activities of the requirements engineering process are:

1. **Feasibility study**: estimate whether user needs can be met with current software and hardware technologies, cost-effectively and within budget.
2. **Requirements elicitation and analysis**: derive the requirements through observing existing systems, discussions with potential users and procurers, and task analysis.
3. **Requirements specification**: translate the gathered information into a document defining the requirements.
4. **Requirements validation**: check the requirements for **realism, consistency and completeness**; errors found must be corrected.

The class notes list elicitation, analysis, specification, validation and **requirements management** as the major activities. These activities interleave rather than following strictly in sequence.

## Methods of gathering requirements

**Interviews** (direct conversations between the requirements engineer and stakeholders), **questionnaires**, **workshops**, **observation**, **document analysis** and **prototyping**.

## Kinds of requirement

- **User requirements** are statements, in natural language plus diagrams, of the services the system should provide and its constraints, understandable without technical knowledge.
- **System requirements** are more detailed descriptions of functions, services and constraints, a starting point for design (sometimes called the functional specification).
- **Functional requirements** state the services the system should provide and how it should react to inputs and situations.
- **Non-functional requirements** are **constraints** on the services, such as timing, development process and standards; they often apply to the whole system (performance, security, availability) and can be more critical than individual functions. Their metrics include speed, size, ease of use, reliability, robustness and portability.

The **software requirements specification (SRS)** is the official statement of what the developers should implement, including user and system requirements. Ideally requirements are clear, unambiguous, easy to understand, complete and consistent.
