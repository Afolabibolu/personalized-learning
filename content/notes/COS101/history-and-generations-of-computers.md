# History and Generations of Computers

## Early devices

The **abacus**, from Asia Minor about 2,500 years ago, is a memory aid with beads on strings, apparently on a **radix of five**. **Al-Khowarizmi** gave us the idea of a written process for doing something, the **algorithm**, which is the basis of software. **John Napier**'s **bones (1617)** made multiplication and division easier; he also invented **logarithms**, the basis of **William Oughtred**'s **slide rule**.

**Blaise Pascal** conceived the **Pascaline** in **1642** to help his tax-collector father. This gear-based mechanical calculator added and subtracted. **Gottfried Leibniz** built the **Stepped Reckoner (1674)**, the first calculator to **multiply and divide**, and documented the **binary number system** used by all modern computers. **George Boole** (1815–1864) invented **Boolean algebra**, used in computer switching.

## Babbage to Hollerith

**Charles Babbage** designed two machines:

- The **Difference Engine** computed polynomial values by finite differences. It was not built in his lifetime; the London Science Museum built one in 1989–1991.
- The **Analytical Engine** was programmable with **punched cards**, with sequencing, branching and looping.

**Ada Byron, Countess of Lovelace**, documented his work and wrote a program for computing **Bernoulli numbers**, so she is called the first programmer. **Herman Hollerith** built an electromechanical **punched-card tabulator** for the **1890 US census**. His Tabulating Machine Company became **IBM**.

## The war years

**Konrad Zuse** built a calculator in 1938. **Alan Turing**'s 1936 paper *On Computable Numbers* described the **Turing machine**, an abstract model of what can and cannot be computed; **Colossus** broke the German Enigma code. **Howard Aiken**'s **Harvard Mark I (1944)** was electromechanical, ran on relays and was 51 feet long; **Grace Hopper** programmed it and coined the term **"bug"**. **ENIAC (1946)**, the first electronic digital computer, used **vacuum tubes**, ran 1,000 times faster than the Mark I, worked in decimal and was programmed by switches and cords.

The **transistor (1947, Bell Labs)** replaced hot, short-lived vacuum tubes. **John von Neumann** gave the **stored-program concept** (EDVAC): programs are stored with data, and binary is used.

## Generations

| Generation | Technology | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st (about 1946–1957) | Vacuum tubes | Machine and assembly language; magnetic drums |
| 2nd (about 1958–1964) | Transistors | High-level languages such as **FORTRAN and COBOL** |
| 3rd (1965–1971) | Integrated circuits (SSI, MSI) | Operating systems and time sharing; IBM 360 family |
| 4th | LSI and **VLSI**, the **microprocessor** | Intel 4004 (1971); IBM PC |
| 5th | VLSI, with artificial intelligence as the aim | |

**Moore's law (1965)**: chip capacity roughly doubles about every two years. Transistor counts went from 2,300 on the 4004 to 42 million on the Pentium IV. **VisiCalc** was the first **killer application**, software so useful that people buy a computer to use it.
