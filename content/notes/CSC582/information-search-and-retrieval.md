# Information Search and Retrieval

## Why literature searching matters

Searching the literature is an essential part of all research. Most resources are now available electronically, but that does not make them easier to retrieve, and searching can be a challenging task. This topic covers the best tools for searching, the logic of building advanced queries, the basic search tags, and efficient searching in PubMed.

## Biomedical literature resources

- **PubMed:** a bibliographic database run by the NCBI that covers a wide range of biomedical literature. It is a service of the National Library of Medicine (NLM) and gives access to over 26 million MEDLINE citations and other life-science journals. It also links to full-text articles and related resources.
- **eBlast:** compares a query set of sentences with a database of text to find similar text. Matching an abstract against every abstract in MEDLINE, for example, finds related papers. It also helps identify expert reviewers, suitable journals and similar publications.
- **Google Scholar:** a specialised Google search engine for academic literature.
- **Scopus:** the largest abstract and citation database of peer-reviewed literature, across all scientific domains.
- **ScienceDirect:** linked to Scopus and Elsevier; searches many journals across all scientific domains.

## Why use PubMed and MEDLINE

- comprehensive, current, up-to-date information
- literature from credible sources
- a controlled vocabulary for indexing and searching
- abstracts and often full text
- filters that narrow a query

## Steps in a literature search

1. Know clearly what information you are looking for.
2. Break your search question into keywords.
3. Identify the main sources of quality data.
4. Decide whether to combine your keywords or search for them separately.

Advanced searches are run at the PubMed site. Example research topics used in class were self-medication as a major cause of drug abuse, immune response in children under five, and the immunological response to *Candida albicans*.

## MeSH terms

**Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)** are the controlled vocabulary of the NLM. Subject specialists read each article and assign MeSH terms that describe its content. As a result, articles on the same subject are grouped together, whatever words the authors used. Looking up the MeSH term for your keywords lets you run a more specific search. For example, searching on a MeSH heading finds articles that use different everyday words for the same idea.

A good search therefore moves from a clear question, to keywords, to the matching MeSH terms, and then combines them with filters. It uses PubMed for biomedical work and Google Scholar, Scopus or ScienceDirect for wider coverage.
